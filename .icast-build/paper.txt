Abstract— Content-based medical image retrieval is frequently evaluated using a fixed gallery, although the availability of relevant cases can vary substantially in practice. This study quantified how chest X-ray retrieval changed as target-pathology prevalence decreased and whether the change differed across pathologies and embedding models. Six ResNet-50 representation conditions were evaluated using NIH ChestX-ray14, SIIM-ACR Pneumothorax Segmentation, and three external chest X-ray collections. For each of cardiomegaly, emphysema, and pneumothorax, a fixed set of 200 queries was searched against 20,000-image galleries containing 5%, 2%, 1%, or 0.5% relevant images. Five gallery samples were evaluated at each setting while the trained checkpoint and query set remained fixed. Precision@10 was the primary endpoint and nDCG@10 assessed ranking position. From 5% to 0.5% prevalence, absolute Precision@10 losses ranged from 0.096 to 0.348 across model-pathology combinations. At 0.5% prevalence, the leading absolute Precision@10 values were 0.016 to 0.066, corresponding to 3.26 to 13.20 times the random-ranking expectation. Within a single fine-tuning run per learned condition, SwAV-SupCon ranked first in all 12 pathology-prevalence conditions and exceeded SwAV-CLS in 10 of 12 globally corrected comparisons. Declining gallery prevalence therefore caused substantial absolute degradation, while the effect of representation learning depended on the target pathology and adaptation objective.
Keywords— content-based medical image retrieval, chest X-ray, Radiography, Thoracic, Information Storage and Retrieval, gallery prevalence, representation learning
I. INTRODUCTION
Content-based medical image retrieval (CBMIR) identifies reference images by comparing their visual representations with that of a query. In embedding-based  systems, images are encoded as feature vectors and ranked by similarity [1], [3]. Unlike a fixed-output classifier, a retrieval system can return examples of categories not explicitly represented in its training labels, provided suitable examples exist in the searchable database; this capability has, for example, been demonstrated in dermatoscopy [6]. Such retrieval can support case-based education, second-opinion reference, and exploration of medical image archives.
Retrieval performance depends on both the representation and the composition of the database, hereafter referred to as the gallery. For target pathology t, target prevalence is defined formally in Equation (1), where Nₜ is the number of gallery images labelled with t and N is the total gallery size. Evaluations involving rare diseases and imbalanced galleries provide evidence about retrieval under scarcity [2], [4], [5]. However, performance at a particular gallery composition does not establish how retrieval quality changes as the target becomes progressively less prevalent.

This question differs from varying the amount of training data or the absolute number of indexed examples while maintaining balanced class proportions. Decreasing target prevalence changes the balance between relevant images and competing gallery items. A controlled evaluation can quantify the sensitivity of retrieval performance to this change and determine whether comparisons between embedding methods depend on the prevalence at which they are evaluated.
This study addresses two questions. First, how does declining target-pathology prevalence affect zero-shot retrieval performance across pretrained and task-adapted  ResNet-50 representations for unseen chest X-ray pathologies? Here, zero-shot means that the three evaluated target-pathology labels were excluded from task-specific fine-tuning; it does not mean that every representation was learned without labels. 'Unseen' likewise refers to absence from the available fine-tuning labels, subject to the annotation-completeness caveat in Section 3.1. Second, within a SwAV-initialized ResNet-50, how does supervised contrastive fine-tuning compare with classification fine-tuning as target-pathology prevalence declines? The study makes three contributions: (1) a prevalence-controlled retrieval protocol that holds query sets, gallery size, and fitted representations fixed; (2) a six-condition comparison across three held-out pathologies; and (3) a direct comparison of classification and supervised-contrastive adaptation from the same SwAV initialization. Precision@k and normalized discounted cumulative gain (nDCG@k) assess the proportion of relevant results and their placement within the retrieved ranking, respectively. Section 2 positions the study in prior CBMIR work, Section 3 describes the protocol, Section 4 presents and interprets the results, and Section 5 concludes the paper.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Representations for Medical Image Retrieval
Embedding-based CBMIR has been investigated using both pretrained feature extractors and representations adapted to medical tasks. Denner et al. [1] compared foundation models for radiological retrieval, including supervised, self-supervised, and vision-language representations. Their evaluation showed that off-the-shelf embeddings can support retrieval across multiple modalities, while performance differs between anatomical and pathological targets. Khun Jush et al. [3] investigated pretrained embeddings for volumetric medical image retrieval through slice aggregation, extending the use of two-dimensional feature extractors to three-dimensional data.
Task-specific representation learning provides another approach. Wickstrøm et al. [7] incorporated CT tissue information into self-supervised learning for liver-image retrieval and reported improvements over their comparison representations. Tschandl et al. [6] examined retrieval using classification-trained features for dermatoscopic images, including disease categories absent from training. Together, these studies motivate comparing pretrained representations and task-specific adaptation; they do not establish a universal advantage for either supervised or self-supervised learning.
Chest X-ray retrieval has also been studied with task-specific radiographic representations. Yu et al. [23] formulated X-ray retrieval as multimodal multitask learning from images and reports, whereas CheXNet [24] established  DenseNet-121 as a strong thoracic-disease feature learner on ChestX-ray14. CheXNet itself is a classifier rather than a retrieval system, so its classification performance does not establish nearest-neighbour retrieval quality. Together, these studies motivate evaluating the retrieved rankings directly rather than assuming that features from a diagnostically effective classifier are necessarily optimal for retrieval.
Efficiency and class imbalance introduce additional retrieval questions. DenseHashNet [25] maps medical images to compact binary codes for efficient similarity search, while Kou et al. [26] explicitly addressed long-tailed image retrieval using attention-guided contrastive hashing. These methods modify the learned representation or hashing objective; the present study instead isolates evaluation-time gallery prevalence while holding continuous embeddings fixed. At substantially larger archive sizes, approximate and compressed similarity search, including GPU implementations based on product quantization [27], can reduce search time and memory at the cost of possible ranking error. Exact cosine ranking was feasible for the 20,000-image galleries used here, so approximation error was not part of the experiment.
B. Scarcity and Gallery Composition
Several studies examine retrieval in settings containing rare categories or imbalanced galleries. Chen et al. [2] evaluated Self-Supervised Image Search for Histology (SISH) on a rare-cancer cohort and galleries formed by combining cohorts. Khun Jush et al. [4] evaluated volumetric radiology retrieval across anatomical structures in imbalanced data. Differences between structures demonstrate heterogeneous retrieval performance, although such comparisons alone do not isolate prevalence from other differences between targets.
Ben Abacha et al. [5] examined heterogeneous CT galleries containing images from different anatomical regions and evaluated relevance at different levels of specificity. Wang et al. [8] evaluated fundus-image retrieval on disease sets with different category coverage, including rare conditions. Such evaluations broaden the range of diseases tested, but changing category coverage does not independently isolate the effect of target prevalence.
A particularly close precedent is the index-size experiment of Denner et al. [1]. Their protocol changes the number of indexed examples per class while preserving balanced class proportions, so absolute gallery support changes but relative prevalence remains constant. In contrast, the present protocol fixes the gallery at 20,000 images and reduces only the selected target's share from 5% to 0.5%, thereby increasing the proportion of competing images. The two designs therefore manipulate different quantities and answer different questions. Consequently, their reported metric values are not numerically comparable  with those in this study; the comparison is conceptual and protocol-level only.
C. Remaining Gap and Study Scope
This narrative, non-systematic review covers rare-disease retrieval, imbalanced gallery composition, and changes in absolute gallery support. Among the studies reviewed, we did not identify a controlled, multi-level reduction in target-pathology prevalence with both the query set and the trained representation held fixed. This motivates the present evaluation without implying that retrieval scarcity has been ignored.
The contribution is a controlled evaluation of retrieval quality as target prevalence decreases across multiple chest X-ray pathologies and selected embedding methods. Conclusions concern the evaluated methods, datasets, and prevalence protocol; they do not establish a general ordering of supervised and self-supervised learning approaches.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. Study Design and Data Sources
This study evaluated the sensitivity of content-based chest X-ray retrieval to decreasing target-pathology prevalence and whether that sensitivity varied across pathologies and embedding models. Three target pathologies were retained after the tuberculosis source-bias exclusion described in Section 3.3: pneumothorax, cardiomegaly, and emphysema. For each pathology, the retrieval database size, query set, and model parameters were held fixed while the proportion of relevant database images was varied.
Data sources were NIH ChestX-ray14 [9], SIIM-ACR Pneumothorax Segmentation [13], the COVID-19 Radiography Database uploaded by Tawsifurrahman [14], [18], the JTIPTJ chest X-ray compilation [15] with a component source documented in [19], the SachinKumar chest X-ray collection [16], [20]–[22], and the tuberculosis collection by Kiran and Jabeen [17]. The COVID-19 Radiography Database is referred to by this name throughout. Its Viral Pneumonia folder was mapped to the broader pneumonia training class because viral pneumonia is a pneumonia subtype. Lung Opacity was excluded rather than mapped to pneumonia because it is a nonspecific radiographic finding that can arise from several conditions. Segmentation masks were excluded. The target pathologies were excluded from the labelled data used for task-specific fine-tuning. Because some external datasets did not provide complete patient identifiers or exhaustive finding annotations, this exclusion refers to their available labels. Dataset-level image counts, label provenance, access terms, and roles in the experiments are summarized in Supplementary Table S11. The residual risk of unlabelled target pathology in the external fine-tuning images could not  be quantified from the released metadata: those folder-organized collections provide a single dataset class rather than exhaustive, radiologist-adjudicated multilabel findings. An image assigned to a common class could therefore contain an unrecorded evaluated pathology. Accordingly, 'held out' and 'unseen' refer only to the available fine-tuning labels, not to verified clinical absence from every training image.

B. Data Preparation and Partitioning
The three common-class collections contained 26,813 image records before deduplication. SHA-256 identified byte-identical files, and a 64-bit discrete-cosine-transform perceptual hash (pHash; ImageHash default hash size of 8) identified resized or re-encoded copies. Only identical hashes (Hamming distance 0) were merged; near matches were not removed. The audit found 258 exact-duplicate groups, 3,602 identical-pHash groups, and 3,332 identical-pHash groups spanning more than one source. Three duplicate groups contained discordant folder labels; for each group, the record occurring first after sorting by source and image path was retained. Collapsing duplicate groups removed 5,776 records and left 21,037 unique radiographs.
The dataset used to train the common-class model was balanced jointly by class and source. For each of the normal, pneumonia, and COVID-19 classes, it contained 569 images from the JTIPTJ collection, 844 from the SachinKumar collection, and 102 from the Tawsifurrahman collection. This produced 1,515 images per class and 4,545 images overall. The data were divided into training and validation sets in an 80:20 ratio using stratification by the combination of source and class. Images assigned to either split were excluded from the retrieval background pool.
NIH ChestX-ray14 contained 112,120 images. A patient was excluded from NIH training, validation, and  background partitions if any study belonging to that patient carried at least one of the three target labels: pneumothorax, cardiomegaly, or emphysema. This patient-level exclusion accounted for 40,954 images. Among the remaining patients, 15% were reserved for the retrieval background; the other 85% were divided into training and validation sets in an 80:20 ratio. The resulting partitions contained 48,280 training images from 18,611 patients, 12,266 validation images from 4,653 patients, and 10,620 retrieval-background images from 4,106 patients. These three image counts sum to the remaining 71,166 NIH images. The same NIH partitions were used for all three NIH-adapted models.
C. Query Sets and Prevalence-Controlled Galleries
A fixed set of 200 queries was constructed for each included target pathology. Pneumothorax queries came from SIIM and the corresponding relevant gallery pool came from NIH, preventing exact query-gallery image overlap across these sources. The available SIIM metadata provided ImageId but not a patient identifier; ImageId was therefore used as an image-level grouping key, and patient-level uniqueness among SIIM queries could not be verified. Cardiomegaly and emphysema queries came from NIH, with at most one query image selected per patient. All images from those query patients were excluded from the NIH retrieval pools.
A dataset-source audit conducted after gallery construction but before the final statistical summaries indicated substantial source bias in the tuberculosis evaluation. Tuberculosis queries and relevant gallery images originated from different collections, so pathology relevance could not be separated cleanly from dataset source. Tuberculosis was therefore excluded as an evaluated target. The fitted models and the independently constructed galleries for the three retained targets were unchanged; summary statistics, ranks, and Holm correction families were recomputed using only pneumothorax, cardiomegaly, and emphysema. Tuberculosis images remained a fixed 2% distractor class in those retained galleries. Pneumothorax queries came from SIIM-ACR whereas the relevant gallery pool came from NIH ChestX-ray14, so source and query-gallery role were not separable for that target. No post hoc source-separability diagnostic was performed; consequently, the pneumothorax evaluation is treated as a cross-source retrieval condition rather than a source-controlled estimate of pathology-specific retrieval performance.
Each gallery contained 20,000 images. Target-pathology prevalence was set to 5%, 2%, 1%, or 0.5%, corresponding to 1,000, 400, 200, or 100 relevant images. For every target, the other three manipulated pathologies—tuberculosis, pneumothorax, cardiomegaly, and emphysema excluding the current target—served as explicit distractor classes and each contributed 400 images (2%). The remaining positions were filled with background images sampled as evenly as  availability permitted across the available sources. For NIH-derived pathology pools, images carrying another manipulated target label were excluded, although unrelated secondary findings were permitted. Manifest assertions verified the exact target count in all galleries; consequently, achieved prevalence equalled the nominal value in every seed (mean and range: 5% and 5%, 2% and 2%, 1% and 1%, or 0.5% and 0.5%).
Five gallery samples were generated for every included pathology-prevalence combination using seeds 0-4, yielding 60 analysed galleries. Five samples were chosen pragmatically to measure sensitivity to gallery resampling within the available computation budget; standard deviations across five samples are consequently imprecise and are interpreted descriptively. The target, non-target pathology, and background pools were resampled between gallery seeds. All six models were evaluated using the same gallery manifests and fixed query sets.
Source counts were identical across the five seeds within each prevalence because source quotas were fixed and only image identities were resampled. For each retained target, the galleries contained 4,686 JTIPTJ background images, 274 SachinKumar background images, 514 normal images from the tuberculosis collection, 400 tuberculosis distractor images, and prevalence-dependent contributions from NIH and the COVID-19 Radiography Database. The complete source counts are reported in Supplementary Table S9.

The other two evaluated pathologies and tuberculosis each contributed 400 distractor images (2% each); the remaining images formed the background pool. D. Representation Models and Fine-Tuning
All conditions used a ResNet-50 backbone [10]. Retrieval embeddings were taken from its 2,048-dimensional global average-pooled representation after replacing the classification layer with an identity mapping. Table 1 summarizes the six representation conditions.

Chest X-rays were converted to grayscale, replicated across three channels, and resized to 224 × 224 pixels. Images were normalized using ImageNet channel means of (0.485, 0.456, 0.406) and standard deviations of (0.229, 0.224, 0.225). Training augmentations comprised horizontal flipping with probability 0.5, rotation within ±7°, random resized cropping with scale 0.90–1.00 and aspect ratio 0.95–1.05, and brightness and contrast jitter of 0.10. Evaluation used only resizing and normalization.
All layers were fine-tuned using AdamW with a learning rate of 10⁻⁴, weight decay of 10⁻⁴, and CosineAnnealingLR scheduling over a maximum of 50 epochs. No learning-rate warmup was used. Classification models used a global batch size of 64. Training continued for at least 10 epochs and stopped when validation loss failed to improve for seven consecutive epochs. Cross-entropy loss was used for the three-class model, whereas binary cross-entropy with logits was used for NIH multi-label classification. Training ran in Kaggle with PyTorch 2.10.0+cu128, automatic mixed precision, and DataParallel across two visible NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs. Python, NumPy, CPU and CUDA Torch generators were seeded with 42; deterministic cuDNN behavior was enabled. Each learned condition was trained once, and the five gallery seeds were not additional training runs.
CNN-Common stopped after 10 epochs; its selected checkpoint was epoch 3 (validation loss 0.0953, validation accuracy 0.9758) and training took approximately 51 minutes. CNN-NIH stopped after 11 epochs; its selected checkpoint was epoch 4 (validation loss 0.1741, validation micro-F1 0.6124) and training took approximately 49 minutes. SwAV-CLS ran for approximately 10 epochs and 71 minutes, but its retained notebook output ends during epoch 6, so the final selected epoch and validation value could not be reconstructed reliably. SwAV-SupCon stopped after 12 epochs; its selected checkpoint was epoch 5 (validation contrastive loss 4.7509) and training took  approximately 90 minutes. Retrieval wall-clock time was not retained.
The SwAV initialization was loaded through the official Facebook Research SwAV Torch Hub repository (facebookresearch/swav:main, model resnet50) and resolved to the ImageNet-pretrained 800-epoch checkpoint swav_800ep_pretrain.pth.tar [11]. For SwAV-SupCon, each batch contained 64 source images and two independently augmented views of each image, giving 128 views per optimization step; this effective contrastive batch was therefore not identical to the 64-image classification batch. A 2,048-2,048-128 projection head was attached during training and discarded before retrieval. The positive-pair weight between two different images was the Jaccard overlap of their NIH label sets, while the two views of the same image received a weight of one. The supervised contrastive temperature was 0.07, following the general supervised contrastive framework [12]. Model selection used the validation loss native to each training objective: classification loss for classification models and contrastive loss for SwAV-SupCon. This avoided using the test retrieval task for checkpoint selection but did not provide a common selection criterion across objectives; model comparisons are therefore conditional on these objective-specific checkpoints.
For a minibatch containing two views of each of B source images, let zᵢ denote the L2-normalized projected representation, A(i) all views except anchor i, and wᵢⱼ the positive-pair weight. The optimized weighted supervised-contrastive objective was

For the paired augmented view of the same source image, wᵢⱼ = 1. For views from different source images with NIH label sets Yᵢ and Yⱼ, wᵢⱼ = |Yᵢ ∩ Yⱼ|/|Yᵢ ∪ Yⱼ|; pairs with no shared labels therefore receive zero weight. The temperature τ was 0.07. Anchors were normalized by their total positive weight Σⱼwᵢⱼ.
E. Retrieval and Evaluation Metrics
Backbone embeddings were L2-normalized, making their dot product equivalent to cosine similarity. Retrieval used exhaustive exact comparison: for every query, similarities to all gallery images were computed and ranked in descending order. Query images were excluded from their corresponding gallery pools, preventing self-retrieval. The historical implementation imposed no explicit secondary rule for exactly equal similarity values, so tied items followed the tensor-ranking order produced by the evaluation implementation. A retrieved image was relevant if its recorded labels contained the query's designated target pathology. Other shared or co-occurring findings did not receive partial relevance.
Precision@10 and normalized discounted cumulative gain at 10 (nDCG@10) were used to assess the proportion of relevant results and their placement within the retrieved ranking, respectively. The fixed cutoff represents a compact returned set and permits direct comparison across prevalence levels. Precision@10 measures how many of those ten results are relevant, while nDCG@10 adds sensitivity to their ordering. mAP and R-precision were not added because the study concerns a fixed, short retrieval depth: mAP emphasizes a much longer portion of the ranking, whereas the R-precision cutoff would change from 100 to 1,000 with the manipulated number of relevant gallery images and would therefore confound depth with prevalence. Precision@10 divided by target prevalence was also reported descriptively as enrichment over the expected precision of random ranking.
P@k = (1/k) Σᵣ₌₁ᵏ yᵣ.    (3)
nDCG@k = [Σᵣ₌₁ᵏ yᵣ/log₂(r+1)] / [Σᵣ₌₁ᵐⁱⁿ⁽ᵏ,ᴿ⁾ 1/log₂(r+1)].    (4)
In Equations (3) and (4), yᵣ indicates whether the image at rank r is relevant and R is the number of relevant images in the gallery. Because every gallery contained at least 100 relevant images, the ideal ranking contained relevant images at all evaluated positions.
F. Statistical Analysis
For descriptive reporting, query scores were averaged within each gallery sample. Means and sample standard deviations were then calculated across the five gallery samples for each model, pathology, and prevalence. These standard deviations represent variability caused by gallery sampling, not training variability. The analysis plan—including the sole reported retrieval depth (k = 10), primary metrics, prevalence levels, primary 5%-to-0.5% contrast, and six model contrasts—was specified before the corresponding results were examined. The later exclusion of tuberculosis following the source-bias audit was a protocol amendment; statistical summaries and multiplicity families were then recomputed for the three retained pathologies without substituting a result-dependent contrast set.
Inferential comparisons were paired by query. Each query's score was first averaged across the five gallery samples, so the tests condition on the observed galleries rather than modelling gallery seed as a random effect. For each model-pathology pair, differences across the four prevalence levels were assessed using the Friedman test, with Kendall's W reported as an effect size. The primary prevalence contrast compared performance at 5% and 0.5% using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The absolute loss was defined as the mean query-level score at 5% minus that at 0.5%, and an unadjusted 95% percentile bootstrap interval was calculated using 10,000 paired query resamples. Holm correction was recalculated across 18 tests  (six models multiplied by three pathologies) separately for Precision@10 and nDCG@10.
Models were compared at each pathology-prevalence condition using a Friedman test. Six planned contrasts reflected the experimental design: CNN-PT versus SwAV-PT, CNN-PT versus CNN-Common, CNN-NIH versus SwAV-CLS, SwAV-PT versus SwAV-CLS, SwAV-CLS versus SwAV-SupCon, and SwAV-PT versus SwAV-SupCon. These contrasts used two-sided paired Wilcoxon tests, paired mean differences, and unadjusted 95% percentile bootstrap intervals. Holm correction was recalculated across the complete family of 72 planned comparisons (six contrasts multiplied by three pathologies and four prevalence levels). Bootstrap intervals were not multiplicity-adjusted; claims of statistical significance therefore follow the Holm-adjusted p-values.
To test whether paired models were differently affected by declining prevalence, query-level loss from 5% to 0.5% was computed for each model. Differences between losses were tested with the paired Wilcoxon procedure and summarized with bootstrap intervals; Holm correction was recalculated across the 18 included pathology-comparison combinations. All tests used a two-sided significance level of 0.05.
IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. Effect of Declining Target-Pathology Prevalence
Retrieval performance decreased consistently as target prevalence fell from 5% to 0.5% (Figures 3 and 4). Across the 18 retained model-pathology combinations, four-level Friedman tests used N = 200 queries and 3 degrees of freedom. For Precision@10, the Friedman χ² statistic ranged from 410.78 to 574.63; for nDCG@10, it ranged from 412.14 to 576.87. Every test remained significant after Holm correction (p < 0.001). Kendall's W ranged from 0.685 to 0.958 for Precision@10 and from 0.687 to 0.961 for nDCG@10. The prevalence ordering was most consistent for pneumothorax (W = 0.918-0.958 for Precision@10).

The absolute decline did not imply convergence to random retrieval. At 0.5% prevalence, the leading Precision@10 values were 0.0163 for cardiomegaly, 0.0335 for emphysema, and 0.0660 for pneumothorax, corresponding to enrichment factors of 3.26, 6.70, and 13.20 over the random expectation of 0.005. Enrichment increased from 5% to 0.5% in 17 of the 18 included model-pathology combinations, indicating descriptively that Precision@10  usually declined by less than the tenfold reduction in prevalence. The exception was CNN-Common on cardiomegaly, whose enrichment decreased from 2.69 to 2.18; this isolated pattern indicates that common-class adaptation did not uniformly preserve relative discrimination for the hardest target, although the experiment does not identify its mechanism. Enrichment is a relative benchmark rather than a substitute for absolute retrieval quality, and its uncertainty follows that of the mean Precision@10 numerator.
The median sample standard deviation across the five gallery samples was 0.0048 for Precision@10 and 0.0058 for nDCG@10; the respective maxima were 0.0189 and 0.0183. These values describe sensitivity to gallery resampling only.
B. Differences Between Embedding Conditions
Model performance differed significantly at all 12 included pathology-prevalence combinations in the Precision@10 omnibus tests. These six-model Friedman tests used N = 200 queries and 5 degrees of freedom; the Friedman χ² statistic ranged from 13.03 to 104.91, with Holm-adjusted p-values from 0.0231 to <0.001. The size of this model effect was pathology-dependent. Kendall's W ranged from 0.013 to 0.038 for cardiomegaly, 0.035 to 0.055 for emphysema, and 0.082 to 0.105 for pneumothorax. Thus, although statistical evidence of model differences was present, the cardiomegaly effects were negligible in magnitude and should not be interpreted as practically actionable model separation.
Across the 12 pathology-prevalence conditions, SwAV-SupCon achieved an overall mean Precision@10 of 0.1291 and mean nDCG@10 of 0.1383. The next-highest model, SwAV-PT, achieved 0.0984 and 0.1063, respectively. SwAV-SupCon had the best mean rank (1.00) and ranked first in all 12 conditions. The globally Holm-corrected planned comparisons identified 26 significant differences among 72 tests. Their direction varied by pathology:
SwAV-PT exceeded CNN-PT for cardiomegaly at 1%, 2%, and 5% and for pneumothorax at every prevalence; no corrected difference was detected for emphysema.
CNN-Common exceeded CNN-PT for pneumothorax at every prevalence, whereas no corrected differences were detected for cardiomegaly or emphysema.
No corrected differences were detected between CNN-NIH and SwAV-CLS, which used the same NIH classification objective with different initializations.
No corrected differences were detected between SwAV-PT and SwAV-CLS.
SwAV-SupCon exceeded SwAV-CLS at all emphysema and pneumothorax prevalences and at 2% and 5% for cardiomegaly; the other two cardiomegaly differences did  not survive correction. It also exceeded SwAV-PT at every pneumothorax prevalence and at 2% for emphysema, with no corrected cardiomegaly difference.
These comparisons do not support a universal advantage for supervised or self-supervised ImageNet pretraining. For the secondary question, they show a consistent numerical advantage for supervised contrastive adaptation over classification adaptation within the evaluated SwAV-initialized ResNet-50: SwAV-SupCon had the higher mean in every pathology-prevalence condition and a significant advantage in 10 of 12 globally corrected comparisons. The two non-significant comparisons were cardiomegaly at 0.5% and 1% prevalence. Thus, the evidence was strongest for emphysema and pneumothorax and less conclusive for cardiomegaly at the two lowest prevalence levels.
At 0.5% prevalence, SwAV-SupCon and SwAV-CLS achieved Precision@10 values of 0.0163 and 0.0133 for cardiomegaly, 0.0335 and 0.0175 for emphysema, and 0.0660 and 0.0446 for pneumothorax. The corresponding Holm-adjusted p-values were >0.99, 5.34 × 10⁻⁷, and 3.17 × 10⁻⁶. Thus, the low-prevalence advantage was statistically supported for emphysema and pneumothorax but not for cardiomegaly.
C. Differences in Absolute Prevalence Sensitivity
Eight of the 18 planned comparisons of 5%-to-0.5% Precision@10 loss remained significant after global Holm correction. SwAV-SupCon had a larger absolute loss than SwAV-CLS for cardiomegaly (difference 0.0308), emphysema (0.0531), and pneumothorax (0.0639), yet SwAV-SupCon remained the highest-performing model at every prevalence for all three pathologies. Similarly, CNN-Common lost more than CNN-PT on pneumothorax (0.0593) while retaining higher Precision@10 at both endpoints.
These results expose an important scale effect. A model that starts with higher Precision@10 has more absolute performance available to lose. Consequently, a larger absolute loss indicates greater absolute prevalence sensitivity, not automatically poorer rare-case retrieval or weaker practical robustness. Endpoint performance and descriptive enrichment are needed to judge retrieval at low prevalence. The bootstrap intervals reported for these contrasts are ordinary 95% intervals and are not multiplicity-adjusted; conclusions about statistical significance therefore follow the Holm-adjusted p-values.
D. Interpretation in Relation to Prior Work
Performance differed substantially by pathology. Pneumothorax showed the strongest retrieval enrichment, followed by emphysema, while cardiomegaly was the most difficult target. This heterogeneity indicates that prevalence alone does not determine retrieval difficulty. Cardiomegaly is commonly assessed through a global relationship between  cardiac silhouette width and thoracic width rather than a single localized texture. Global average pooling collapses the final spatial feature map into channel averages and discards explicit information about where activations occurred and how distant anatomical boundaries relate. That representation may therefore be less suited to cardiothoracic geometry than to findings with more localized visual signatures. This is a mechanistic hypothesis rather than a demonstrated explanation: no spatially preserving pooling method, segmentation signal, or localization analysis was evaluated. Differences in visibility, acquisition, label noise, heterogeneity, and co-occurrence may also contribute.
The findings extend prior CBMIR evaluations by isolating gallery prevalence while keeping the query set, gallery size, and fitted representation fixed. Denner et al. [1] showed that retrieval quality varies across representations and target types; the present results additionally show that the magnitude of prevalence sensitivity and the separation between representations vary across the three included pathologies. Evaluating multiple targets avoids inferring general behaviour from a single disease.
The results are consistent with the observation that classification-trained representations can retrieve categories absent from their training labels [6]. CNN-Common, CNN-NIH, and SwAV-CLS retrieved held-out target pathologies above random expectation. SwAV-SupCon's consistent advantage over SwAV-CLS for cardiomegaly, emphysema, and pneumothorax is compatible with evidence that clinically informed representation learning can improve medical-image retrieval [7].
The increase in enrichment for most conditions complements the expected absolute decline. It suggests that the learned rankings often retained discrimination relative to the increasingly unfavourable class balance. This should not be interpreted as clinical adequacy: Precision@10 at 0.5% remained low in absolute terms for several conditions, and the study did not evaluate whether the returned sets improved diagnostic decisions or clinician workflow.
E. Scope and Limitations
Each learned condition was represented by one fitted checkpoint, so the results do not estimate variability across independent retraining. The five gallery seeds instead quantify gallery-sampling variability. All models used a ResNet-50 backbone, so the results do not establish architecture-independent properties of the training objectives. Classification and supervised-contrastive checkpoints were selected using different objective-specific validation losses rather than a shared retrieval criterion, so their comparison is conditional on that selection design. A subsequent audit identified substantial source confounding in the tuberculosis evaluation, leading to its exclusion as a target; this exclusion reduces pathology coverage and should be considered when assessing generalizability.  Pneumothorax also used SIIM queries with an NIH relevant-image pool, leaving source perfectly aligned with query-gallery role. Because no source-separability diagnostic was performed, the comparatively strong pneumothorax results cannot be attributed to pathology similarity independently of dataset-source cues and should be interpreted only as performance under the evaluated cross-source configuration. NIH ChestX-ray14 labels were derived automatically from radiology reports and may contain label noise; because NIH supplied adaptation data and relevant-image pools, such noise can affect both learned representations and evaluation relevance. Horizontal flipping during training imposed left-right invariance despite the laterality and anatomical orientation of chest radiographs, which may have disadvantaged laterality-dependent findings. The remaining datasets, acquisition pipelines, and label completeness also differed between targets. Because the external common-class datasets lack exhaustive multilabel adjudication, the direction and magnitude of target-label contamination are not identifiable from the available metadata. Any such contamination could expose target-related visual features during fine-tuning and weaken a strict clinical interpretation of zero-shot retrieval; the results therefore support only the narrower claim that the target labels were absent from the recorded fine-tuning annotations. Relevance was binary and label-based; co-occurring findings received no partial credit, and no radiologist assessed visual or clinical similarity. The lowest prevalence, 0.5%, still provided 100 relevant images in a 20,000-image gallery. These galleries permitted exhaustive ranking, whereas substantially larger clinical archives may require approximate indexing that introduces additional retrieval error. Behaviour at still lower prevalence or with fewer absolute relevant cases remains untested.
V. CONCLUSION
Chest X-ray CBMIR was strongly sensitive to declining target-pathology prevalence: reducing prevalence from 5% to 0.5% significantly lowered Precision@10 and nDCG@10 for every included model-pathology combination. The magnitude of the decline and the separation between models were not uniform. Within the evaluated SwAV-initialized ResNet-50, supervised contrastive adaptation produced higher mean Precision@10 than classification adaptation in every condition and significantly exceeded it in 10 of 12 globally corrected comparisons. Low-prevalence performance remained above random expectation for all included conditions, but absolute Precision@10 was limited for several targets.
Lower target prevalence reliably reduced retrieval quality, but the practical severity of the reduction and the ordering of embeddings depended on the pathology. Evaluations at only one gallery composition can consequently give an incomplete account of model  performance. CBMIR benchmarks should therefore report performance at a minimum of two clinically relevant gallery-prevalence levels. Future work should extend the protocol to additional architectures and lower absolute target counts, improve cross-source control, and include clinically assessed relevance.
